# MASTER INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENT ROADMAP
## An integrated curriculum for self-mastery, understanding reality and strategic agency

### I. THE PURPOSE

The objective of this curriculum is to develop the intellectual capabilities needed to:

1. Understand yourself and human behavior.
2. Reason independently and evaluate information accurately.
3. Understand how society, institutions and economic systems operate.
4. Recognize incentives, power structures and underlying mechanisms.
5. Identify assumptions, inefficiencies and overlooked opportunities.
6. Anticipate possible changes and consequences.
7. Make better decisions under uncertainty.
8. Translate understanding into deliberate action.

This is not primarily an academic philosophy curriculum. Philosophy provides foundational questions and frameworks, while psychology, economics, history, political science, technology and other disciplines provide additional explanations and evidence.

The goal is not to become familiar with many thinkers. It is to develop the ability to think independently using the knowledge they provide.

---

# II. THE THREE PILLARS

### PILLAR 1 — SELF-MASTERY

Develop the ability to understand, regulate and direct yourself.

Core subjects:
- Human behavior and psychological mechanisms.
- Character and virtue.
- Emotional regulation.
- Metacognition and self-awareness.
- Identity and behavioral change.
- Judgment and personal responsibility.

Primary intellectual foundations:
Aristotle, Epictetus, Nietzsche, William James and modern psychology.

Existing resource:
The previously developed eight-part personal development curriculum.

### PILLAR 2 — UNDERSTANDING REALITY

Develop the ability to understand how individuals, groups, institutions and larger systems operate.

Core subjects:
- Human nature.
- Social psychology.
- Epistemology.
- Political philosophy.
- Economics.
- Sociology.
- History.
- Science and technology.

Primary intellectual foundations:
Hobbes, Hume, Machiavelli, Locke, Rousseau, Adam Smith, Hayek, Popper and Kuhn.

### PILLAR 3 — STRATEGIC AGENCY

Develop the ability to recognize opportunities, anticipate consequences and act effectively.

Core subjects:
- First-principles reasoning.
- Systems thinking.
- Decision theory.
- Game theory.
- Strategic positioning.
- Innovation.
- Entrepreneurship.
- Foresight and execution.

Primary intellectual foundations:
Sun Tzu, von Neumann, Herbert Simon, Schumpeter, Peter Thiel and modern decision science.

# III. THE SEQUENTIAL CURRICULUM

## PHASE 0 — INTELLECTUAL FOUNDATIONS

### Objective
Develop the basic reasoning and learning capabilities needed to study difficult subjects without becoming overwhelmed by unfamiliar terminology, arguments or competing explanations.

### Subjects

1. Critical thinking and logical reasoning.
2. Arguments, premises and conclusions.
3. Deductive and inductive reasoning.
4. Cognitive biases and common reasoning errors.
5. Epistemology: what knowledge is and how we justify beliefs.
6. Scientific reasoning and evidence evaluation.
7. Reading comprehension and argument reconstruction.

### Prerequisites
None. This is the entry point.

### Recommended books
- *The Demon-Haunted World* — Carl Sagan.
- *Asking the Right Questions* — M. Neil Browne and Stuart M. Keeley.
- *The Scout Mindset* — Julia Galef.
- *The Problems of Philosophy* — Bertrand Russell.

### Learning objectives
By the end, you should be able to:
- Identify the central claim of an argument.
- Separate evidence from interpretation.
- Recognize assumptions.
- Distinguish confidence from certainty.
- Identify logical weaknesses.
- Change your mind when better evidence appears.
- Explain an idea in your own words.

### Exercises
- Reconstruct arguments from philosophical passages.
- Identify hidden assumptions in everyday statements.
- Compare two explanations of the same event.
- Identify what evidence would change your mind.
- Distinguish correlation, causation and coincidence.

### Assessment
Explain an unfamiliar argument, identify its premises, evaluate its evidence and construct a reasonable objection.

## PHASE 1 — HUMAN NATURE AND BEHAVIOR

### Objective
Understand the psychological and social mechanisms behind human behavior.

This phase is foundational because economics, politics, business, relationships and institutions are all influenced by human behavior.

### Subjects

1. Human motivation.
2. Needs, desires and self-interest.
3. Emotions and decision-making.
4. Cognitive biases.
5. Status and social comparison.
6. Cooperation and competition.
7. Trust, reputation and reciprocity.
8. Social conformity and group behavior.
9. Identity and belonging.
10. Individual differences and personality.

### Prerequisites
Phase 0: reasoning, evidence evaluation and basic understanding of psychological claims.

### Recommended books
- *The Righteous Mind* — Jonathan Haidt.
- *Influence* — Robert Cialdini.
- *Thinking, Fast and Slow* — Daniel Kahneman.
- *The Social Animal* — Elliot Aronson.

Supplementary philosophical works:
- *Leviathan* — Thomas Hobbes.
- *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* — David Hume.

### Learning objectives
- Understand why people behave differently under different circumstances.
- Recognize the influence of incentives and social pressures.
- Understand how status and reputation affect decisions.
- Distinguish stated motivations from possible underlying motivations.
- Recognize when your own psychological tendencies distort judgment.
- Understand both cooperation and competition.

### Exercises
- Analyze the behavior of characters in literature.
- Observe how incentives influence behavior in workplaces.
- Compare what people say they value with what their actions suggest.
- Identify status dynamics in organizations.
- Analyze how group pressure changes individual decisions.

### Assessment
Given an unfamiliar social situation, identify the possible motivations, incentives, psychological mechanisms and alternative explanations.

## PHASE 2 — POWER, POLITICS AND INSTITUTIONS

### Objective
Understand how societies organize themselves, distribute authority and maintain systems of power.

This phase explores the difference between how institutions are supposed to operate and how they actually operate.

### Subjects

1. Political philosophy.
2. Authority and legitimacy.
3. Power and influence.
4. Hierarchies and institutions.
5. Formal versus informal power.
6. Political realism.
7. Reputation and alliances.
8. Governance and law.
9. Bureaucracy and organizational behavior.
10. Institutional incentives.
11. Collective action and coordination.

### Prerequisites
Phase 0 and Phase 1.

You should have a basic understanding of arguments, human motivation, social behavior and incentives.

### Recommended books
- *The Prince* — Niccolò Machiavelli.
- *Politics* — Aristotle.
- *The Social Contract* — Jean-Jacques Rousseau.
- *The Road to Serfdom* — Friedrich Hayek.
- *The Origins of Political Order* — Francis Fukuyama.

Additional works:
- *The Dictator's Handbook* — Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and Alastair Smith.
- *The 48 Laws of Power* — Robert Greene (read critically as a popular synthesis, not as a scientific or universal theory).

### Learning objectives
- Understand different theories of political authority.
- Identify how power is acquired, distributed and maintained.
- Recognize the difference between formal authority and actual influence.
- Understand why institutions behave in particular ways.
- Analyze the incentives of political and organizational actors.
- Recognize how rules create advantages and disadvantages.

### Exercises
- Analyze a workplace as an institutional system.
- Identify who controls resources, information and decisions.
- Compare the public justification for a policy with its likely incentives.
- Examine historical examples of institutional success and failure.
- Analyze how an organization's rules influence its members.

### Assessment
Choose an institution and explain its power structure, incentives, rules, beneficiaries, constraints and vulnerabilities.

## PHASE 3 — ECONOMICS AND VALUE CREATION

### Objective
Understand how economic systems function, how value is created and why certain individuals, businesses and industries become disproportionately successful.

### Subjects

1. Scarcity and opportunity cost.
2. Supply and demand.
3. Marginal thinking.
4. Incentives and unintended consequences.
5. Competition and market structures.
6. Comparative advantage.
7. Information asymmetry.
8. Externalities.
9. Capital and resource allocation.
10. Entrepreneurship.
11. Innovation and creative destruction.
12. Market inefficiencies.

### Prerequisites
Phases 0–2.

Especially important:
- Human incentives.
- Social coordination.
- Institutional rules.
- Logical and quantitative reasoning.

### Recommended books
- *The Wealth of Nations* — Adam Smith (selected sections).
- *Basic Economics* — Thomas Sowell.
- *The Undercover Economist* — Tim Harford.
- *Economics in One Lesson* — Henry Hazlitt.
- *The Innovator's Dilemma* — Clayton Christensen.

Advanced and supplementary:
- *The Road to Serfdom* — Friedrich Hayek.
- *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* — Joseph Schumpeter.

### Learning objectives
- Understand how markets coordinate behavior.
- Recognize the importance of incentives.
- Identify opportunity costs and trade-offs.
- Understand how businesses create and capture value.
- Recognize market failures and inefficiencies.
- Understand how innovation changes competitive environments.
- Distinguish revenue, profit, value and economic advantage.

### Exercises
- Analyze a local industry.
- Identify unnecessary costs in a business process.
- Compare competing business models.
- Identify who creates value and who captures it.
- Explain how a change in one incentive might affect an entire market.
- Identify an underserved customer need.

### Assessment
Analyze an industry, explain its economic structure, identify inefficiencies and develop a plausible opportunity for creating value.

## PHASE 4 — KNOWLEDGE, SCIENCE AND PARADIGM SHIFTS

### Objective
Understand how knowledge develops, how accepted beliefs change and how people recognize possibilities that established frameworks overlook.

### Subjects

1. Epistemology.
2. Scientific methodology.
3. Falsifiability.
4. Paradigms and scientific revolutions.
5. Assumption testing.
6. First-principles reasoning.
7. Conventional wisdom.
8. Technological change.
9. Disruptive innovation.
10. Uncertainty and incomplete information.
11. Intellectual humility.

### Prerequisites
Phase 0 is particularly important. Phases 1–3 provide useful examples of how knowledge and assumptions operate in human and economic systems.

### Recommended books
- *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* — Karl Popper.
- *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* — Thomas Kuhn.
- *The Beginning of Infinity* — David Deutsch.
- *The Art of the Soluble* — Peter Medawar.
- *Zero to One* — Peter Thiel and Blake Masters.

### Learning objectives
- Understand how scientific knowledge is evaluated.
- Distinguish established knowledge from speculation.
- Recognize assumptions underlying accepted explanations.
- Understand how paradigms influence what people notice.
- Evaluate whether an apparently novel idea is genuinely useful.
- Identify situations where existing models may no longer explain reality.

### Exercises
- Challenge an established assumption in an industry.
- Find historical examples of accepted beliefs being overturned.
- Identify what evidence would falsify a hypothesis.
- Compare competing explanations of technological change.
- Examine the difference between contrarianism and evidence-based disagreement.

### Assessment
Identify an accepted assumption, construct an alternative hypothesis, establish what evidence would support or reject it, and evaluate its practical implications.

# IV. THE INTEGRATION PHASE

After completing the foundational sequence, the subjects should no longer remain separate.

The goal is to develop an integrated way of understanding reality.

Consider a single problem: an established industry is being disrupted by new technology.

Analyze it through multiple disciplines:

- Psychology: How will customers respond?
- Economics: Where is value created and captured?
- Political philosophy: Which institutions and regulations influence the industry?
- Systems thinking: What dependencies and bottlenecks exist?
- Epistemology: Which assumptions might be wrong?
- Strategy: How will competitors respond?
- Innovation: What new opportunities are emerging?

The ability to combine these perspectives is more valuable than knowing each discipline in isolation.

### Final integration project

Choose an industry, organization, social problem or technological development.

Produce an analysis containing:

1. Historical background.
2. Human motivations and incentives.
3. Economic structure.
4. Institutional environment.
5. Underlying assumptions.
6. Systems map.
7. Strategic actors and interactions.
8. Potential future developments.
9. Opportunities and risks.
10. A proposed course of action.

The analysis must distinguish established facts, reasonable inferences, hypotheses and speculation.

# V. PROGRESSION MODEL

Use a hybrid progression rather than requiring complete mastery of one subject before encountering another.

### Level 1 — Foundation
Understand the essential vocabulary, concepts and mechanisms.

### Level 2 — Working competence
Explain the subject, identify it in real situations and apply its basic frameworks.

### Level 3 — Integration
Connect it with other disciplines and evaluate competing explanations.

### Level 4 — Advanced understanding
Study original works, difficult arguments, exceptions, controversies and limitations.

The normal progression is to establish Levels 1 and 2 before moving forward. Return to Levels 3 and 4 as later subjects expose additional connections and complexities.

Do not confuse familiarity with mastery.

---

# VI. LEARNING AND ASSESSMENT STANDARDS

A subject is sufficiently understood for progression when you can:

1. Explain its central ideas without notes.
2. Define its important concepts accurately.
3. Explain why its mechanisms operate.
4. Reconstruct its important arguments.
5. Identify assumptions and limitations.
6. Apply its ideas to unfamiliar situations.
7. Compare it with competing explanations.
8. Recognize when its frameworks are inappropriate.

Not every subject requires equal depth.

Foundational reasoning, human nature, economics and systems thinking deserve repeated attention because they support many later subjects.
